1.01 Integers and opposites (booklet p. 3)

Teacher notes

  • Key misconception. Reading the − in −5 only as "take away", so −5 feels like an action rather than a place; and thinking "opposite" means a nearby or equal number.
  • Why? (pair 1) Why is "below sea level" negative?
    Sea level is 0. Below 0 is negative.
  • Why? (pair 2) What is the opposite of 0? Why?
    0. It is 0 steps from 0, so there is no other side.
  • Why? (pair 3) Read −(−7) aloud, using the word "opposite".
    "The opposite of negative seven."
  • Check (hinge). What is the opposite of −8? Answer: B.
    A (−8): thinks "opposite" means the same number. Re-teach with the Big idea picture: same distance, other side.
    B (8): correct.
    C (0): confuses opposites with a zero pair (−8 + 8 = 0). Point out: the opposite is a number, not the sum.
    D (−7): moved one step, not to the other side. Show the two equal jumps from 0.
  • Next. B: page 2. A, C or D: redraw the Big idea on the board, walk out from 0 both ways, then redo Your turn 2 before page 2.
  • Think first. Accept "−2", "B2" or "two down" and turn them into −2. Point out that the lift buttons often show −1, −2.

1.02 The number line (booklet p. 5)

Teacher notes

  • Key misconception. Counting marks instead of gaps (answers one too many), and subtracting the sizes (4 − 3 = 1) when two numbers are on opposite sides of 0.
  • Why? (pair 1) Only every second mark has a number. How did we still find it?
    Each gap is 1. Count gaps from a labelled mark.
  • Why? (pair 2) Why do we add the two parts?
    The trip passes 0. Steps to 0 plus steps from 0 make the whole trip.
  • Why? (pair 3) How can you check that −3 is halfway?
    It is 5 steps from −8 and 5 steps from 2.

    D (−1): subtracted and kept a minus. Re-teach Example 2 on the number line.

1.03 Comparing and ordering integers (booklet p. 7)

Teacher notes

  • Key misconception. Magnitude confusion: "−9 is bigger than −4 because 9 > 4". Students compare the digits and ignore the sign.
  • Why? (pair 1) Sam says −9 is larger because 9 is more than 4. What would you tell Sam?
    The − means left of 0. −9 is further left, so it is smaller.
  • Why? (pair 2) How do you remember which way < and > point?
    The open side faces the larger number.
  • Why? (pair 3) Where would your answer sit on a number line?
    Left to right, in the same order.
  • Check (hinge). Which is the smallest? Answer: C.
    A (−2): chose the "smallest digit" (magnitude confusion). Place all four on a number line together.
    B (0): thinks 0 is the smallest number. Remind: negatives are less than 0.
    C (−9): correct.
    D (−5): partly there; check each pair on a number line.
  • Next. C: page 2. A or D: put all four numbers on one number line on the board, and read left to right. B: short reminder that negatives are below 0 (thermometer).
  • Think first. Nearly all will say −8°C. Ask: "So which number is smaller?" This is the Big idea: colder = lower = smaller.

1.04 Adding integers (booklet p. 9)

Teacher notes

  • Key misconception. Treating −2 + 5 as "−(2 + 5)" (the symmetric error), and carrying "two negatives make a positive" over from multiplication into addition.
  • Why? (pair 1) We moved right. Why?
    We added a positive number (+3), and adding a positive moves right.
  • Why? (pair 2) 2 − 5 also moves 5 left from 2. What does that tell you about 2 + (−5)?
    2 + (−5) is the same as 2 − 5. Adding a negative is the same as subtracting.
  • Why? (pair 3) Tom says −4 + (−3) = 7. What went wrong?
    Tom used "two negatives make a positive". That rule is about multiplying, not adding. Adding a negative moves left, so −4 + (−3) = −7.
  • Check (hinge). Work out −2 + 5 Answer: B.
    A (−7): added the sizes and kept the minus sign ("symmetric" error). Re-teach with Example 1 on the number line.
    B (3): correct.
    C (−3): moved the wrong way, or did 5 − 2 and kept the minus. Act it out: start at −2, walk 5 steps right.
    D (7): ignored the negative sign. Check they read −2 as a place left of zero (Words box).
  • Next. Most students B: go on to page 2. Students with A, C or D: a short re-teach at the board with Examples 1 and 2 on the number line (walk it out), then Your turn 1 and 2 again before page 2. Students who finish Mixed practice: Go further.

1.05 Subtracting integers (booklet p. 11)

Teacher notes

  • Key misconception. Treating − (−3) as − 3, so 4 − (−3) = 1. Students see one minus sign and ignore the other.
  • Why? (pair 1) Why is 2 − 6 negative?
    We took away more than we had, so we went past 0.
  • Why? (pair 2) Why does subtracting a negative move right?
    Subtracting is adding the opposite. The opposite of −4 is 4, so we add 4.
  • Why? (pair 3) Ali says 3 − (−2) = 1. What went wrong?
    Ali ignored the negative. 3 − (−2) = 3 + 2 = 5.

1.06 Multiplying integers (booklet p. 13)

Teacher notes

  • Key misconception. Believing a negative × a negative is negative ("negative means less"), and, from the other side, using "two negatives make a positive" when adding.
  • Why? (pair 1) Why is 3 × (−2) negative?
    It is 3 groups of −2. Adding negatives gives a negative.
  • Why? (pair 2) Is −5 × 4 the same as 4 × (−5)? Why?
    Yes. Swapping the order does not change a product.
  • Why? (pair 3) Page 2 has a pattern that shows why − × − is positive. What does it show?
    Each time the first number goes down 1, the answer goes up 4. Past 0 the answers are positive.

1.07 Dividing integers (booklet p. 15)

Teacher notes

  • Key misconception. Keeping the negative when both numbers are negative (−20 ÷ (−4) = −5), usually from not linking ÷ to × facts.
  • Why? (pair 1) How does the × fact help with the ÷?
    Division undoes multiplication, so the × fact gives the ÷ answer.
  • Why? (pair 2) How can you check that 28 ÷ (−4) = −7?
    Multiply back: −7 × (−4) = 28.
  • Why? (pair 3) Jo says −30 ÷ (−6) = −5. What went wrong?
    Same signs give a positive. The answer is 5.
  • Check (hinge). Work out −20 ÷ (−4) Answer: B.
    A (−5): kept the negative. Check by multiplying back: −5 × (−4) = 20, not −20.
    B (5): correct.
    C (−16): subtracted: −20 − (−4). Check the operation.
    D (80): multiplied. Check the operation.
  • Next. B: page 2. A: model "multiply back to check" on three examples. C or D: underline the operation before answering.
  • Think first. Write −24 ÷ 4 = −6: the share of a debt is itself a debt. Link to Example 1.
